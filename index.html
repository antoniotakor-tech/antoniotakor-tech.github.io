Calm Navigation Companion

Get me there fastest → Get me there without wrecking me
Role
Solo — start to finish
Evidence
Shipped, then audited in production
Made
A live, installable web app
Status
Public. Anyone can open it right now
Who I spoke to
Nobody yet. Built from desk research and from walking the same streets at different hours. The assumption it broke was my own: I thought shipping was the finish line. Reading my own code back found three faults a mockup never shows. Next: putting it in front of people who are sensory-sensitive, and building the next version from what they say.
What every map does
Every other map→ Shortest time→ Busiest streets→ You arrive wrecked
What this one does
This one→ Quieter streets→ A few minutes longer→ You arrive fine

Every mapping app optimises for time. If crowds and noise drain you, the fastest route is the one that costs you most. So I built it.

Then I read my own code back and found what a mockup would never have shown me.

  • Researched by walking the same streets at different hours, and reading how routing engines weight road class.
  • Built in plain HTML, CSS and JavaScript — about 15,000 lines, no framework, nothing in the stack I cannot maintain alone.
  • Shipped as an installable PWA with the routing key proxied server-side, so it never reaches the browser.
  • Audited my own code afterwards and found three faults a mockup would never surface. All three fixed.

02 Research & concept 2026 Solo · Amsterdam

The Return Gap

Who is making the mess → Why the search fails
Role
Solo — research, framing, concept
Evidence
Eight street interviews, my own observation of the bins, and twelve interventions benchmarked across two countries
Made
A collection model and a costed first pilot
Status
Proposed. The pilot has not been run
Who I spoke to
Eight people in the street, over two days — Amsterdam Centraal, a canal-side park, along the canals. I went expecting complaints about rubbish. Not one person raised it unprompted. What broke the assumption came last: a resident of seventeen years said the bag gets torn open and half the time there is nothing in it.
What happens now
Home→ Street bin→ Bag torn open to look→ Often nothing in it

The search is blind, so it has to wreck the bag to find out. That is the mess.

What I proposed
Home→ Caught inside the building→ Returned for the deposit→ Money split three ways

Catch it at the front door and there is nothing left in a bag worth opening.

Bins across Amsterdam get torn open for deposit containers, and the city started pulling bins out in response. Nobody had asked what the person opening the bag was actually doing.

The mess is not carelessness; it is what a search leaves behind when it fails.

1

Discover

Two days of interviews in the street.

2

Define

Nobody raised it on their own. Everyone blamed a different group. Nobody had ever reported it.

3

Develop

Every attempt I could find anywhere acts on the public bin.

4

Deliver

Catch the containers inside the building instead. One building, three months, one number to watch.

03 Product manager 2025–26 Brand partner, via my degree

Purylock

Keep people dry in the rain → Get people dry after it
Role
Product manager, in a team
Evidence
Interviews with commuters, concept tested with them
Made
A defined concept, priced
Status
Pitched back to the brand partner
Who I spoke to
Commuters at Amsterdam Centraal, then the OBA library when the station sample started repeating itself. Storyboard-tested afterwards with about five classmates and commuting friends. I expected them to complain about getting wet. What they actually described was the four hours after arriving: a jacket that never dries, shoes still wet at lunch, and nowhere to put any of it.
Where the brief was aimed
Caught in the rain→ Arrive at work→ Sit in wet clothes→ Four hours ruined

The brand existed to stop step one. Nobody was doing anything about steps three and four.

Where the pain actually was
Caught in the rain→ Arrive at work→ Hang it in the locker→ Dry by lunch

One unit in the lobby serves the whole building, every day it rains.

A Dutch weather-gear brand, an open brief, no product category and no spec. My job was to make the direction defensible with evidence instead of taste.

All of the pain sat on one moment: arrival.

“It’s not the rain. It’s sitting in a wet jacket until half two.”

A commuter at Amsterdam Centraal, on the second day of interviews

That sentence moved the whole project. The brand existed to stop people getting wet; nobody was working on the four hours afterwards, which is where the day actually gets ruined. One shared drying unit in a lobby serves a whole building every time it rains.

Classmates tell you it is interesting. Only strangers tell you they want it.

04 Concept & validation 2025–26 Team of six

GrandPod

An app for lonely older people → A human on the other end
Role
Concept and validation, in a team of six
Evidence
26 concepts narrowed down, tested with people over 75, then assessed live
Made
A working physical prototype and a browser MVP
Who I spoke to
Around twenty people while narrowing 26 concepts down to one, plus observation of how older people use public space and testing with real participants over 75. The assumption that broke was the team's: we wanted to build an app for people who are defined by being shut out of apps.

Loneliness and digital exclusion among people over 75 who want to keep living on their own. The team's instinct was an app.

Voice first. A real person on the line, the machine behind it. These are people who are offline by definition. An app makes the barrier the product. Same reason AI does not go at the front: a synthetic voice asking a fraud-wary 80-year-old to trust it starts out losing.

We tested it with a cardboard box and a phone menu standing in for the real thing, first with people over 75 and then in front of assessors. What we learned there is why fear of being scammed decides what this group accepts from anyone, human or machine.
